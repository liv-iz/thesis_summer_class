Abstract

This thesis presents Ukweli Wetu, a design research framework for activating community archives to increase the utility of assets intended to serve the public. While many public and community-facing assets hold extensive archival records, these materials are often preserved without being meaningfully integrated into how the assets function in the present. This work investigates how archival narratives can move beyond documentation to become active components of sociotechnical systems.

Ukweli Wetu is a situated, tech-agnostic framework structured around six steps: identifying an asset’s immutable layer, defining intended utility with the community, selecting a medium that interfaces with that layer, imprinting community narratives, activating those imprints through designed interactions, and iterating based on use and feedback. The framework treats archival contribution as an input rather than an endpoint, emphasizing activation as the mechanism through which archives gain practical value.

The framework was developed and tested through participatory design interventions at the Asian Art Museum in San Francisco. Using Webbased augmented reality accessed through personal smartphones, community narratives were anchored to artworks through image recognition and layered lighting conditions. Participants contributed text, audio, images, video, and drawings through scavenger hunts, animation workshops with children, and quilting workshops with elders. These contributions were stored in a cloud-based database and activated through AR-mediated prompts, pathways, and participatory tours designed to encourage reflection, dialogue, and connection. Informal feedback and observational evaluation informed iterative refinement of the system.

Rather than claiming generalizability, this thesis demonstrates the framework’s viability within a museum context where community meaning and institutional narratives coexist in tension. Ukweli Wetu is offered as a design research contribution that foregrounds archival activation as a strategy for increasing public utility while maintaining institutional stewardship. The thesis concludes by inviting designers and communities to test the framework across other shared assets to explore where its principles hold, where they break, and how dormant archives might be reactivated in service of collective needs.

Introduction

Public assets such as museums, libraries, land, and cultural artifacts are often positioned as shared resources meant to serve broad communities. However, the narratives that shape how these assets are understood, accessed, and valued are frequently controlled by institutions with disproportionate power. In recent years, political and institutional interventions into museums, educational curricula, and public history have made visible how narrative control can be used to reshape historical interpretation, suppress certain forms of knowledge, and redefine the public meaning of shared assets [1], [2]. These shifts demonstrate that narrative is not neutral. It directly affects who an asset serves and how its utility is distributed.

At the same time, many of the truths that institutions overwrite already exist. Community stories, oral histories, lived experiences, and creative interpretations are often archived through formal collections, community led efforts, or informal preservation practices [3], [4]. Despite their presence, these archives frequently remain dormant. They are stored rather than used, documented rather than activated, and preserved without meaningfully shaping how an asset functions in the present. Prior scholarship in archival studies has shown that preservation alone does not guarantee representation, agency, or public impact [5], [6]. This gap raises a central question for design research: how can archival material move beyond passive preservation to actively increase the utility of assets intended to serve communities?

This thesis explores that question through a design research lens. It asks how community narratives can be anchored to shared assets in ways that resist erasure and then activated through socio technical systems that increase relevance, participation, and accountability. Rather than treating archiving as an end point, this work approaches it as a starting condition for new forms of interaction, interpretation, and use.

To investigate this, I conducted a situated design research project at the Asian Art Museum in San Francisco, an institution with some discrepancies between curatorial authority and community meaning. Through participatory workshops, augmented reality interventions, and visitor driven activities, I explored how community generated narratives could be imprinted onto artworks and activated to reshape visitor engagement. From this process emerged a tech agnostic framework that outlines a sequence of steps for identifying durable-

attachment points, defining intended utility, selecting appropriate media, imprinting community truths, activating those imprints, and iterating over time.

This thesis presents that framework, the museum based prototype through which it was developed, and reflections on its potential and limitations. It does not claim universal applicability. Instead, it positions the framework as a design research contribution that shows promise within contexts where community assets exist in tension with institutional control.

Background

This work sits at the intersection of design research, archival studies, and participatory practice. Across these domains, scholars have consistently shown that archives and narratives are not neutral repositories of truth, but socially constructed systems shaped by power, access, and institutional priorities [7], [8]. Decisions about what is preserved, how it is interpreted, and who controls access to archival material directly influence whose histories are made visible and whose are rendered marginal.

Archival theorists have long argued that dominant institutions often define historical records in ways that privilege official perspectives while excluding or flattening lived experience. Trouillot describes this process as the production of silences, where power operates at every stage of historical creation, from what is recorded to how it is remembered [9]. Derrida similarly frames the archive as a site of authority, where control over documentation shapes future meaning and legitimacy [10]. These perspectives establish that archives do not merely reflect history, but actively participate in shaping it.

In response to these dynamics, community archive scholars have emphasized the importance of participatory, community led archival practices. Caswell and Flinn argue that community archives emerge precisely because institutional archives fail to represent or protect marginalized narratives [5], [11]. Christen extends this work by demonstrating how Indigenous communities develop protocols that embed cultural values, consent, and relational accountability into archival systems themselves [12]. Together, this scholarship reframes archives as relational infrastructures rather than static collections, emphasizing stewardship, care, and shared authority.

Within design research, parallel conversations have emerged around participation, co creation, and power. Early participatory design work by Ehn positioned users as co designers with situated knowledge rather than passive recipients of systems [13]. Sanders and Stappers further developed this approach by articulating co creation as a shift in who participates in design and how value is generated [14]. More recently, Costanza-Chock has argued for design justice, emphasizing that design processes must explicitly address power asymmetries and center those most affected by the outcomes [15]. These perspectives collectively argue that inclusion alone is insufficient without structural-

mechanisms that support equitable participation and long term agency.

Speculative and critical design traditions also inform this work by challenging assumptions about what design produces and for whom. Dunne and Raby position design as a tool for questioning dominant narratives and imagining alternative futures [16]. Escobar extends this critique by advocating for design approaches that recognize multiple worldviews and resist universalizing solutions [17]. These frameworks encourage designers to work with complexity and contradiction rather than resolve them prematurely.

While these bodies of work offer strong critiques and conceptual tools, there remains a gap in how community narratives and archives are operationalized within shared assets. Many participatory and archival projects focus on collection and preservation, but less attention is given to how archived material can be activated to reshape how an asset functions in everyday use. This thesis builds on existing scholarship by exploring how archival practices can be integrated into socio technical systems that directly influence asset utility, interaction, and meaning over time.

This project is motivated by a growing recognition that narrative control over public assets is not only symbolic, but materially consequential. Decisions about how assets are framed, interpreted, and contextualized shape who feels welcome, whose knowledge is legitimized, and how an asset functions in civic life. Recent political and institutional interventions into museums, educational curricula, and public history have made visible how easily narratives tied to shared assets can be reshaped to align with dominant ideologies, often at the expense of community held truths [1], [2], [18]. These shifts highlight the fragility of public meaning and the vulnerability of assets that lack mechanisms for community accountability.

At the same time, many communities already hold rich archives connected to these assets. Oral histories, cultural memory, artistic expression, and lived experience persist despite institutional erasure [3], [6], [12]. However, these archives are often treated as static records rather than active resources. They are preserved without being allowed to meaningfully influence how assets operate in the present. As a result, communities may technically be represented while remaining structurally excluded from shaping how an asset serves them. This disconnect suggests that preservation alone is insufficient. What is missing are systems that translate archived truths into ongoing utility.

My motivation for this work is rooted both in this broader political context and in my own experiences moving through cultural institutions as spaces that claim public service while often privileging institutional authority. Museums in particular occupy a contradictory position. They present themselves as sites of education, connection, and inspiration, yet their curatorial structures frequently centralize expertise in ways that marginalize visitor interpretation and lived cultural knowledge [19], [20]. While many museums have adopted participatory language and community engagement initiatives, these efforts often stop short of allowing community narratives to meaningfully shape interpretive systems or long term institutional memory.

The Asian Art Museum in San Francisco presented a compelling site to explore these tensions. As an institution committed to representing Asian and Asian American art, it operates within complex histories of cultural translation, diaspora, and power. During my engagement with the museum, I observed moments where visitors offered personal,-

cultural, or historical insights about artworks that were not reflected in official interpretive materials. In some cases, there were mismatchings of information from lived cultrure visitors and authoritative museum figures, where community knowledge remained informal, ephemeral, or disengaged. These observations raised questions about how museums might better honor the knowledge already present within their audiences.

Design offers a unique opportunity to intervene in this space. Participatory design and co creation practices provide methods for engaging communities as active contributors rather than passive recipients [14], [15]. At the same time, socio technical systems such as augmented reality create opportunities to layer meaning onto existing assets without physically altering them. These affordances suggest the possibility of designing systems that anchor community narratives to durable aspects of an asset while remaining flexible, revisable, and responsive over time.

This thesis is motivated by the belief that design can help bridge the gap between archival preservation and lived utility. By exploring how community narratives can be imprinted onto shared assets and activated through participatory systems, this work seeks to test whether archives can function as living infrastructure rather than static repositories. The urgency of this inquiry lies in the increasing visibility of narrative control as a tool of power and the need for design approaches that support communities in sustaining their truths within contested spaces.

This thesis followed a design research approach grounded in situated inquiry, participatory design, and iterative prototyping. Rather than beginning with a fixed solution, the work unfolded through cycles of observation, technical experimentation, and reflection within a real institutional context. The Asian Art Museum in San Francisco served as the primary site of investigation, enabling the framework to emerge through engagement with an existing public asset and its communities.

The project began with exploratory research focused on understanding how visitors currently engaged with artworks, interpretive materials, and one another. This included observational research within galleries, informal conversations with visitors, and discussions with museum staff. These early insights informed an initial hypothesis that community narratives, if anchored to durable aspects of an asset and activated through designed systems, could increase that asset’s utility for the people it is intended to serve.

The first operational step of the framework involved identifying the immutable layer of the asset. In the museum context, most assets were physical artworks. Their visual appearance was identified as their most durable and institutionally protected characteristic. This visual layer became the anchor point for all subsequent technical and design decisions, ensuring that narrative imprints were tied to a feature unlikely to be altered or removed over time.

With this anchor established, I worked with museum stakeholders to define the intended utility of the artworks within this context. These discussions clarified shared goals such as fostering inspiration and connection through Asian and Asian American art. These goals were further specified to guide design decisions, with inspiration defined as personal relevance and reflection, and connection defined as active exchange of ideas and experiences among visitors.

To engage the visual layer of the artworks without physical modification, augmented reality was selected as the primary medium. Two AR platforms were used: 8th Wall for browser based WebAR experiences and Spark AR for specific prototyping needs. Visitors accessed AR content on their personal smartphones by scanning QR codes placed at certain stations. This access model avoided the need for app downloads and aligned with museum constraints around-

hardware and installation.

AR experiences were anchored to artworks using a combination of image recognition and contextual triggers. Image based recognition allowed AR content to be attached directly to the visual surface of an artwork, while additional contextual logic supported varied interactions across different gallery environments. This hybrid anchoring strategy increased robustness and flexibility across lighting conditions and visitor movement patterns.

Community narratives were collected through a series of participatory activities designed for different age groups and engagement styles. These included a scavenger hunt with reflective prompts, animation workshops with children, and a quilting workshop with elders. Participants contributed narratives in multiple formats, including text, audio recordings, photographs, videos, and drawings. All contributions were stored in a cloud based database and associated with specific artworks and prompts.

Activation of these imprints occurred through AR overlays that surfaced community narratives in situ. Visitors encountered these narratives alongside prompts inviting reflection, response, or creative contribution. This transformed archived material from static records into interactive elements that shaped how visitors moved through and engaged with the museum space.

Evaluation and iteration were supported through a combination of interviews, surveys, observation notes, and informal verbal feedback from visitors and museum staff. These inputs informed adjustments to prompt wording, interaction flow, and content presentation. Over time, repeated application and refinement of these steps led to the articulation of a generalized, tech agnostic framework abstracted from the museum context.

The framework and its application were later synthesized into a printed and digital zine that functions as a design artifact, a dissemination tool, and a participatory extension of the work. In parallel, exploratory research began around geofencing and land based applications as a future direction for testing the framework beyond institutional interiors.

Outcome

The outcome of this thesis consists of three interconnected components: a design research framework, a museum based socio technical prototype, and a public facing zine that extends the work beyond the original site. Together, these components demonstrate how community narratives can be anchored to shared assets and activated to increase their utility for the communities they are intended to serve.

The primary outcome is the Ukweli Wetu framework, a tech agnostic sequence of design steps developed through iterative application within the museum context. The framework consists of six stages: identifying the immutable layer of an asset, defining intended utility, selecting an appropriate medium, imprinting community narratives, activating those imprints through interaction, and iterating over time. While developed in a museum setting, the framework is articulated abstractly to allow testing in other contexts where community assets exist in tension with institutional control.

The framework was instantiated through a working prototype at the Asian Art Museum. In its final form, the prototype consisted of augmented reality experiences tied to selected artworks across multiple galleries. Visitors accessed these experiences by scanning QR codes with their personal smartphones, launching browser based AR interfaces built using 8th Wall and Spark AR. AR overlays were anchored to the visual surfaces of artworks through image recognition and contextual triggers, aligning with the identified immutable layer.

When activated, the AR experiences surfaced community generated narratives associated with each artwork. These included written reflections, audio recordings, images, videos, and drawings contributed by prior participants. Rather than functioning as static annotations, these narratives were presented as entry points for reflection and engagement. Visitors encountered prompts that encouraged personal interpretation and, in some cases, invited them to contribute additional narratives. New contributions were stored in a cloud based archive and became part of the evolving system.

The outcome also included a set of participatory programs integrated into the system. These consisted of a scavenger hunt guiding visitors through themed pathways, animation workshops where children responded creatively to artworks, and a quilting workshop-

that supported reflection through material practice. Each program generated narrative content that was incorporated into the archive and surfaced through the AR layer, reinforcing the relationship between physical participation and digital activation.

Evaluation of the final prototype drew on interviews, surveys, observation notes, and informal feedback. Visitors reported deeper engagement with artworks when encountering layered community perspectives and reflective prompts. Museum staff noted alignment with institutional goals around inspiration and connection, while also recognizing the value of visitor driven meaning making.

A final outcome of the project is the Ukweli Wetu zine, produced in both printed and digital formats. The zine compiles the framework into an accessible guide intended for use beyond the museum. It functions as a design artifact, a dissemination tool, and a participatory extension of the work, inviting readers to test the framework within their own shared assets.

Discussion

This project set out to explore whether community narratives, when intentionally anchored and activated, could increase the utility of assets intended to serve the public. Through the development and testing of the Ukweli Wetu framework within a museum context, several key insights emerged about the role of archives, participation, and design in contested institutional spaces.

First, the project demonstrates that archives can function as active infrastructure rather than static repositories. Much of the archival literature emphasizes preservation, representation, and memory as ends in themselves [5], [9], [10]. While these functions are essential, the outcomes of this work suggest that archives gain additional value when they are operationalized within systems that directly shape interaction and use. By embedding community narratives into the visitor experience through augmented reality, archived material influenced how people moved through the museum, how they interpreted artworks, and how they related their own experiences to what they encountered. This supports Caswell’s argument that archives are most powerful when they are oriented toward community use and agency rather than institutional custody alone [5].

Second, the framework highlights the importance of anchoring narratives to immutable layers of an asset. By tying community narratives to the visual surfaces of artworks, the system leveraged a layer that is both culturally significant and institutionally protected. This design decision reduced the risk of narrative detachment and increased the durability of the imprint. This finding extends archival and participatory design scholarship by offering a concrete strategy for resisting erasure without requiring institutional overhaul. It also aligns with Derrida’s observation that authority over the archive shapes what persists over time [10], while suggesting that designers can intervene at the level of attachment rather than ownership.

Third, the project reinforces the need for participatory processes that go beyond consultation. Drawing on participatory design traditions [13], [14] and design justice principles [15], Ukweli Wetu positioned community members as contributors to meaning making rather than as audiences to be informed. The workshops and activities demonstrated that when participants understood both the asset and the system they were contributing to, the resulting narratives were more situated,-

reflective, and diverse. This supports Escobar’s call for design approaches that recognize multiple ways of knowing and being [17].

At the same time, the project surfaced important limitations. The framework depends on the presence of a definable community with a meaningful relationship to the asset. In contexts where community boundaries are unclear or highly contested, determining who should contribute to the archive requires careful governance. Additionally, while augmented reality enabled non-invasive narrative layering, it introduced barriers related to device access, digital literacy, and attention. These constraints highlight that the framework is not medium dependent, but medium sensitive.

The museum context also shaped what was possible. While the framework showed promise in this setting, it does not claim universality. Its transferability depends on factors such as institutional openness, asset stability, and community willingness to engage. This reflects broader critiques in design research that caution against assuming that participatory methods alone can resolve structural power imbalances [15], [16].

Finally, the project reframes the role of design in archival contexts. Rather than positioning design as a tool for representation or visualization alone, Ukweli Wetu treats design as a means of structuring relationships between narratives, assets, and use. In doing so, it contributes a framework that bridges archival theory and design practice, offering a way to test how archives might actively serve communities in the present.

Conclusion

This thesis began with a simple but pressing question: how can archival material move beyond preservation to actively increase the utility of assets intended to serve communities? In response, this work explored the role of design in mediating relationships between narratives, institutions, and public assets. Through a situated design research project at the Asian Art Museum, it tested whether community narratives, when anchored to durable aspects of an asset and activated through participatory systems, could meaningfully shape how that asset is experienced and used.

The resulting Ukweli Wetu framework represents the primary contribution of this thesis. Developed through iterative experimentation, the framework offers a structured yet flexible approach for identifying immutable layers of an asset, defining intended utility with communities, selecting appropriate media, imprinting narratives, activating those imprints, and iterating over time. Rather than proposing a universal solution, the framework demonstrates promise within contexts where community assets exist in tension with institutional authority. Its value lies not in standardization, but in providing a repeatable way to ask better questions about power, preservation, and use.

The museum based prototype showed that community narratives can function as more than contextual annotations. When activated through socio technical systems, these narratives influenced visitor engagement, interpretation, and participation. Archived material became part of the lived experience of the space, inviting reflection and contribution rather than passive consumption. This suggests that archives can serve as living infrastructure, shaping present day interaction while remaining grounded in community knowledge.

At the same time, this work acknowledges its limits. The framework depends on the presence of engaged communities, institutional willingness to collaborate, and thoughtful governance around inclusion and care. It does not resolve structural inequalities on its own, nor does it eliminate the risks of co option or exclusion. Instead, it offers a design mediated way to surface these tensions and work within them deliberately.

Ultimately, Ukweli Wetu argues for a reframing of archival practice-

within design. It positions archives not as endpoints of memory, but as starting points for utility, connection, and accountability. By inviting others to test, adapt, and challenge the framework in their own contexts, this thesis treats its conclusions not as final answers, but as an opening. What remains unresolved is not whether archives matter, but how widely and thoughtfully they can be activated in service of the communities they are meant to support.
