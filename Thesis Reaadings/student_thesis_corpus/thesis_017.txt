Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated strong potential as partners in diffi­

cult logical reasoning tasks, including decision-making [24, 45], programming [ 20, 25,

30, 42], problem solving [22, 36] and mathematical theorem proving [20, 23, 26, 33].

A long-term vision for human–AI collaborative reasoning is to develop an end-to-end

reasoning system that maximizes accessibility by enabling free-form natural language

interaction, supported by formally verified reasoning on the backend. Figure 1 shows the

user interaction we envision for an end-to-end machine-checked reasoning system, with

auto-formalization and auto-informalization used to translate natural language to formal

language and vice versa. The autoformalized content is processed by a theorem prover to

verify user inputs, but feedback is given in natural language. We consider this system in

the case of an educational tutoring scenario for math proofs.

However, we know that users often struggle to articulate their intended behavior in

natural language in ways LLMs can reliably interpret. Prior works have documented such

challenges in parallel fields such as assisted programming [20, 21, 25 , 30], and other

task-oriented conversational agents [22, 44]. This means that a key step towards our

vision requires building an accurate autoformalizer. Such an autoformalizer must also be

faithful, i.e., it must follow the intent and logical path of a human user [29, 33].

While theorem-proving tools and formal languages such as Lean [10], Rocq [18] and

Isabelle [34] are gaining increasing adoption, their user base is largely expert users. This

is because these languages are challenging and not necessarily aligned to how humans

naturally think about proofs [37]. In this work, we focus on mathematical proofs in a

tutoring context where the human-written natural language reasoning that is fully free-

form without any compromise or deliberate articulation. Can we accurately and faithfully

autoformalize such content into Lean?

We focus on Lean in this paper because of its popularity and widespread adoption, much

larger than the alternatives, but we believe similar results would hold for any of the lan­

guages.

Background 02

Challenge in autoformalization

The gulf of execution [31] for auto-formalization remains substantial. While some

success has been achieved in auto-formalization [ 3, 23, 29, 33, 35, 39, 41 ], current

models still struggle to fully understand real human input, since they often focus on proof

completion, not faithfulness and are trained on polished benchmarks. Moreover, autofor­

malizer outputs are often difficult for humans to parse and interpret.

Theorem proving languages are “low-resource” languages [ 27]. This scarcity is reflected

in existing auto-formalization benchmarks, which contain only polished, textbook-style

natural language and provide a single natural language proof for each problem (e.g.,

ProofNet [ 6 ], MiniF2F [46], Putnam-AXIOM [15], Lean-Workbook [43]) or are gener­

ated through back-translation of formal statements, and thus lack human diversity [ 13,

33, 40, 43]. On the other hand, student proofs in casual learning contexts can be frag­

mented, nonlinear, implicit, and conveyed through loosely structured natural language

[17]. In order to have truly accessible systems, used by both expert and novice users, we

must broaden our perspective on auto-formalization to be able to handle input beyond the

standard benchmarks, and this paper represents a first step in that direction.

Challenges in Aligning Natural Language Input in Human-AI Col­

laborative Programming Tasks

The misalignment between human-friendly natural language and the kind that LLMs

can interpret reliably extends beyond mathematical auto-formalization. In AI-assisted

programming, multiple studies have highlighted the challenges novice programmers face

in learning how to prompt effectively to communicate their intent [20–22 , 25, 30]. Prior

work found that only a small portion of naturalistic utterances are effective in guiding

code generation, and users often feel they must learn the model’s “syntax” [20, 22 , 25].

User’s input are always underspecified thus LLM need to make assumptions in order to

execute user’s logic [21]. Moreover, novice programmers must learn to articulate pro­

gram behavior at an abstraction level that aligns with what the model can interpret [25,

30].

To address these issues, Liu and Sarkar [25] proposed grounded abstraction matching,

where the user’s input is first mapped to a formal representation and then translated back

into natural language for user confirmation — mitigating abstraction mismatches. Jiang

[20] developed an inline AI-assisted programming tool and proposed several design

strategies to address ambiguity in user input. Other studies [20, 42] have also found that

users were open to more constrained syntax or design affordances that confirm word

recognition, viewing these as more helpful for generating reliable outputs than allowing

unrestricted natural language input.

While prior work has largely focused on addressing the abstraction gap between natural

language and formal representations as a unified challenge, our approach demonstrates

that, within a narrower context, ambiguity can be decomposed into distinct types — and

systematically resolved through targeted reformulations. Another key difference lies in

the tolerance for ambiguity: in general AI-assisted programming, it is often acceptable to

generate multiple interpretations and let users select the correct one. In contrast, certain

contexts — including the educational setting of our research — require disambiguation to

the extent that the model and the user are fully aligned, with only one plausible interpre­

tation of the input remaining.

If we aim to use LLMs to make machine-checked reasoning accessible to novices with

no prior knowledge of theorem provers, we must better understand where and why the

process breaks down when humans write proofs in the way they naturally intend. In par­

ticular, what forms of reasoning can humans write, read, and understand, but that LLMs

fail to translate into machine-checkable artifacts?

In this work, we focus on the auto-formalization aspect of this system: we explore the

unspoken logic’ in human reasoning, i.e. the misalignment between the natural language

seen in pen- and paper- proofs and the rigorous natural language proofs required by AI

models for accurate auto-formalization with an aim to design a pre-processing system to

aid the LLM in understanding the human.

We aim to (1) classify the misalignment that arises in this context and (2) explore how

human-written natural language can be interpreted to enhance the success of auto-formal­

ization. (3) design interface which facilitate verifiability of LLM interpretation, fostering

transparent communication between the user and LeanTutor.

Our main contributions are below:

1. Our formative study analyzes auto-formalization of student-written natural language

proofs to create a detailed taxonomy of the specific misalignment that can arise

between human-written natural language proofs and the text required for successful

auto-formalization. We discuss why these mismatches occur, how they affect LLM

outputs, and what they reveal about the broader challenges in human-AI collabora­

tive reasoning tasks.

2. We develop a natural language pre-processor to reduce ambiguitiy based on the

formative study.

3. We evaluate the pre-processing system by comparing to a baseline autoformalizer

and empirically characterizing the ambiguity types the pre-processor can and cannot

mitigate.

4. We design and evaluate the interaction paradigm for future human-AI interactive

co-reasoning which supports transparency in what AI can and cannot understand.

Formative Study Design: Characterizing Ambiguities in Natural

Language Proofs

To understand how and why human-written natural language mathematical proofs fail to

autoformalize correctly, we conducted a formative study using 24 student-written proofs

drawn from three undergraduate-level mathematics exams. We used a baseline auto­

formalizer to formalize all student proofs into Lean, then compared the outputs against

human-written Lean formalizations, to extract steps that were valid for human readers

but led to auto-formalization errors (unable to compile or unfaithful). Using Reflexive

Thematic Analysis [8], we grouped these failing natural-language steps by recurring

ambiguity patterns, yielding a categorization of ambiguity types that drive human-model

misalignment. The full analysis pipeline is shown in Figure 2.

1. Data Collection and Preparation

In our analysis, we consider three different exam problems from popular undergraduate

engineering courses. The three selected exam questions QA, QB, QC spanned a variety

of concepts: Basic Linear Algebra (QA, QB) and Discrete Mathematics and Probability

(QC). They varied in proof strategy: direct (QA), contradiction (QB), and mixed (QC),

and they varied in content type: QA was equation-focused, while QB and QC emphasized

text-based reasoning. The questions also differed in difficulty, based on correct response

rates across all submissions in the class (QA:64%, QB: 2%, QC: 52%). We randomly

selected 24 natural language proofs from correct student answers (8 per question).

2. Method

All 24 student-written proofs (312 steps) were digitized using optical character recogni­

tion (OCR) (GPT-4o [19 ]) with manual correction of recognition errors, segmented into

steps based on visual layout and semantic cues to approximate the student’s reasoning

flow, and passed to the baseline autoformalizer to produce Lean code (baseline auto-for­

malization). The first author then manually constructed reference Lean formalizations

that faithfully reflected each student’s intended reasoning and compared them against the

baseline auto-formalization. We recorded two failure types in the baseline auto-formal­

ization: (1) code that failed to compile (lack of correctness) and (2) code that misrepre­

sented the student’s intent (lack of faithfulness), both reflecting misalignment between

natural language and the model’s interpretation. Formalizing each standard solution took

approximately four hours per question, and each student proof required about one addi­

tional hour to formalize and compare. We then conducted a Reflexive Thematic Analysis

[8 ] of the natural-language steps that produced auto-formalization errors. Following this

method, the first author labeled ambiguities on a subset of steps, iteratively refined a set

of ambiguity categories with clear definitions, and then labeled the remaining steps. This

analysis revealed recurring ambiguity patterns in auto-formalization, which we detail in

the following sections. The full analysis pipeline is shown in Figure 2.

Fig. 2. This figure shows the process for the formative study. The handwritten student proof is transformed to LATEX using OCR (and is manually checked for accuracy). This LATEX input is then provided to an LLM-based autoformalizer that generates Lean-4 code. Natural Language Pre-processing System

The natural language pre-processing system transforms free-form input into a form more

interpretable by LLMs by addressing the four ambiguity categories identified earlier. Its

output is a natural language proof with minimal known ambiguity across these categories.

The pipeline applies five layers of automated processing:

Before auto-formalization:

• Layer 1: Reference Resolution detects pronouns and vague phrases, replacing them

with explicit referents.

• Layer 2: Operation Classification labels each proof step as an assertion or declaration

(and the specific type of declaration).

• Layer 3: Antecedent/Consequent/Evidence Identification extracts the logical compo­

nents of assertions.

• Layer 4: Granularity Supplement applies predefined expansion functions to supple­

ment skipped reasoning steps that commonly cause granularity-related failures.

The fifth and last layer comes after auto-formalization:

• Layer 5: Syntax Repair corrects minor formatting and syntactic issues.

Errors from each layer are manually corrected to ensure fully correct input to the next layer for evaluation purposes. We include a fifth post-process­ ing layer for syntax correction after the Lean code is generated.

Outcomes

Ambiguities in Human-Written Natural Language That Make Aut­

oformalization Unreliable

Ambiguities are clustered into four ambiguity types:

Operation ambiguity (24 cases out of 312 student steps): This ambiguity type captures

the confusion around identifying whether a student statement is a declaration (declaring a

new fact to be true in our context) or an assertion (a transformation that brings the current

state closer to the goal). A human reader is able to infer this information from contextual

knowledge, but our baseline system struggled to do so.

Antecedent/Consequent/Evidence (ACE) ambiguity (33/312): ACE Ambiguity refers

to the cases when a proof step lacks explicit markers for one of the three components in

an assertion step. These components are: (1) the antecedent: the starting expression (un­

solved goal, hypothesis, or known fact), (2) the consequent: the derived conclusion, and

(3) the evidence: the justification linking them. An example can be found in D.3. Human

readers rely on conventional formats (e.g., antecedent on the left, consequent on the right,

joined by an equals sign) and contextual knowledge. However, the autoformalizer must

infer all components based solely on the proof text as none are explicitly marked.

Reference Ambiguity (11/312): Reference ambiguity captures cases where a proof step

uses pronouns (e.g., it, they, them) or referential phrases (e.g.,similarly, as mentioned

before). In cases where students used such expressions, instead of clearly stating the ref­

erent, the autoformalizer failed to recover the original meaning (examples in D.1).

Granularity Ambiguity (50/312): Granularity ambiguity describes cases where students

omitted intermediate steps that are required in a formal proof, but not expected in an in­

formal proof. Common cases included: omitting low-level knowledge or presuppositions

or using referential phrases like “in a similar way” to point to one of the earlier steps in

the proof.

An additional Syntactic/Miscellaneous category captured 60 non-ambiguity errors. For

example, rw [h1] at h2 rewrites h1 into h2, but Lean provide multiple substitution tactics

which make the correct choice theorem dependent; these errors stem from syntax, not NL

ambiguity.

Discussion

A long-term vision for human–AI collaborative reasoning envisions users interacting

with AI systems in natural language, while formal verification occurs unobtrusively in

the background. A crucial step toward realizing this vision is the analysis and resolution

of ambiguity in the natural language channel. The ambiguity types we examined here

are grounded in mathematical contexts, but any human–AI reasoning system is likely to

engage in a similar process: identifying domain-specific ambiguities and addressing them

through automation or design interventions—such as interface constraints, pre-process­

ing, or reverse translation—that are tailored to the characteristic of the ambiguity.

Implications for Pure Natural Language Human–AI Collaborative

Reasoning Tools

One persistent challenge in such systems is the opacity of what the AI “understands.”

Prior studies in AI-assisted programming have shown that novice users often struggle to

articulate their intent in a way the model can correctly interpret [ 31 ]. In contexts like

AI-assisted programming where the AI’s output includes formal code, users—despite

facing a steep learning curve—can still learn to refine their prompts by observing which

inputs yield better outputs. This trial-and-error feedback loop, while imperfect, provides a

pathway for users to gradually develop effective prompting strategies.

Even when the AI’s outputted formal code is available, users may not necessarily be

well-positioned to inspect it for correctness, as in “vibe coding” [39 ]. In these circum­

stances, carefully flagging poorly-reconciled ambiguity is an overriding concern: worse

than code that is obviously incorrect is code that appears at a surface level to be correct—

or at least appears plausibly correct—but does not actually reflect user intent. Such code

may lead to cryptic errors or even short-circuit users’ intuitions for where to debug sys­

tems that do not work as desired, because they may have been inappropriately assumed

by users to be correct.

Further, when formal code is completely hidden from users (for usability reasons, or

because the user may not be proficient in the formal language but wants to use verified

reasoning)—as in a pure natural language reasoning tool—the stakes are much higher.

Consider a scenario where a student interacts with an AI math tutor that uses autofor­

malization to verify the student’s logic and responds with natural language feedback

(such as LeanTutor [34 ]). In this setting, there are two paths that may lead to the system

marking the response as incorrect. The system may mark a response as incorrect because

the student’s logic is genuinely flawed, or simply because the model failed to interpret

the intended meaning due to ambiguity. In such a case, having feedback for the user that

allows the user to verify that the system “interpretation” is true to the user’s intent will be

valuable.

Our analysis shows that reducing ambiguity in student-written natural language signifi­

cantly improves autoformalization accuracy when the underlying logic is correct. This

finding suggests that it is indeed possible to distinguish between truly incorrect reasoning

and valid reasoning that fails due to misinterpretation. Our system and the interface are

proof-of-concept designs which we use to demonstrate such ideas.

Conclusion

Large language models make it increasingly plausible to build end-to-end reasoning

systems where users work in natural language while formal verification happens on the

backend. This paper takes a step toward that vision by examining where autoformaliza­

tion breaks down on the kind of proof text students actually write: fragmented, implicit,

and shaped by human conventions rather than machine requirements. Through a forma­

tive study of 24 student-written proofs, we identified recurring sources of human–model

misalignment and organized them into a taxonomy of four ambiguity types—operation,

antecedent/consequent/evidence (ACE), reference, and granularity ambiguity—along

with a separate set of syntactic and miscellaneous errors. Together, these categories clar­

ify why a baseline autoformalizer can fail even when the underlying reasoning is correct

and intelligible to human readers.

Building on this analysis, we developed a natural language pre-processing system that

systematically reduces known ambiguity before autoformalization. The pipeline applies

layered transformations—reference resolution, operation classification, ACE identifica­

tion, and granularity supplement—followed by optional syntax repair after compilation

feedback. Our evaluation shows that this pre-processing substantially improves autofor­

malization outcomes on the same student proofs, increasing both correctness (successful

compilation) and faithfulness (alignment with student intent). At the same time, the

evaluation reveals clear limits: some ambiguity persists because it depends on domain

knowledge, long-range context, or non-local proof structure that cannot be reliably recov­

ered from text alone.

These findings motivate a shift from purely backend ambiguity resolution to interactive,

user-facing support. Rather than silently “fixing” student language, LeanTutor’s interface

surfaces what the system identifies as ambiguous and what edits it proposes, allowing

users to verify the model’s interpretation and intervene when necessary. This interaction

paradigm is especially important in educational contexts, where incorrect feedback may

stem either from flawed reasoning or from misinterpretation of correct reasoning. By

coupling ambiguity-aware pre-processing with transparency-oriented interface design, we

demonstrate a practical path toward more accessible, trustworthy machine-checked rea­

soning tools that respect how humans naturally write proofs while still meeting the strict

requirements of formal verification.
